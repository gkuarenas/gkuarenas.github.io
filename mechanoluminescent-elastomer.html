Mechanoluminescent Sensing Skin

A light-emitting silicone lattice for visuotactile sensing, with signal extraction and image processing for contact differentiation.

Overview

A mechanoluminescent skin glows where it deforms. Most camera-based visuotactile sensors need dedicated lighting or printed markers to read a soft skin's deformation, and that adds parts and drifts out of alignment over time. This sensing skin removes that need: a phosphor-embedded silicone lattice emits its own light under contact, so a camera can read the deformation directly.

Role: designed the black-housing camera module that observes the skin, and built the software that extracts and processes its emission signals. The sensing skin itself was designed by the co-author team, credited in the linked paper.

Problem & Approach

Problem: Camera-based visuotactile sensors usually need dedicated lighting or careful optical calibration to read a soft skin's deformation. This adds parts, adds assembly steps, and drifts over time from wear or misalignment.

Approach: The co-author team molds a 30 × 30 mm silicone lattice embedded with ZnS:Cu,Al phosphor, which emits light when it deforms and needs no external light source. Guillaume designs a black-housing camera module that mounts the lattice on an acrylic window and observes it, then builds software that records the emission on video and extracts intensity-time signals for two contact modes: normal pressing and shear sliding.

Key Features

  • No external light source: the phosphor-embedded lattice emits its own light under contact
  • Hollow-cell lattice geometry concentrates strain at the rods and junctions, where emission is strongest
  • Two contact modes tested: normal pressing and shear sliding, each recorded and processed separately

Results & Outcomes

  • Pressing and shear produced visibly distinct emission shapes: localized spots for pressing, elongated streaks for shear, confirmed by the extracted intensity-time signals
  • The lattice told the two contact modes apart with no active illumination and no printed markers
  • This is a preliminary, qualitative study. The team applied contact forces by hand and did not measure them
